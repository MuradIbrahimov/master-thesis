# Discussion — section draft

## What the regime models establish

The empirical evidence is strong that oil-return magnitudes vary over time. Squared-return dependence and ARCH-LM rejections precede model choice, and the two-state Markov fits identify materially different conditional standard deviations in both Brent and WTI. The Brent M1 low/high estimates of 1.575 and 3.450 percentage points, and WTI M1 estimates of 1.800 and 4.445, make the labels economically intelligible as quiet and turbulent **fitted** periods. They do not establish that the market literally switches between two observable states. M2's three ordered Brent volatility levels add retrospective detail, but its essentially zero high→low transition and missing standard error make ordinary transition-coefficient inference unreliable. [Diagnostics](../results/tables/02_arch_lm.csv), [Brent states](../results/tables/03_m1_regimes.csv), [WTI states](../results/tables/06_regime_summary.csv), and [M2 audit](../results/tables/03_correction_summary.md) substantiate this interpretation.

## Association is narrower than prediction

The primary Brent M5 associates higher lagged GPR with entry into high volatility: its fitted low→high probability rises by about 3.57 percentage points from the training 10th to 90th GPR percentile, with a conditional delta interval above zero. VIX is more clearly associated with *persistence* of an existing high-volatility state: its M5 high→high probability rises by about 19.30 percentage points over the analogous VIX range. These associations are conditional on the fitted Gaussian observation equation, retained observations, specified lags, training period and other predictor. The logit parameters are not marginal probabilities, and percentile scenarios are more interpretable than isolated coefficients. [Transition contrast table](../results/tables/06_transition_scenarios.csv) and [transition figure](../results/figures/04_transition_scenarios.png) give the estimates.

The evidence also limits those statements. WTI's M5 GPR entry interval includes zero, as does the Brent 2007-start interval; WTI M5 and short-sample Brent VIX persistence intervals are wide. Some transition probabilities are near deterministic, so asymptotic covariance approximations warrant care. Correlation between lagged news, financial uncertainty and oil-market states is compatible with common shocks, delayed measurement and changing market conditions. Neither model timing nor a significant fitted contrast identifies an exogenous geopolitical-risk effect. [Cross-sample contrasts](../results/tables/06_transition_scenarios.csv), [stability diagnostics](../results/tables/06_numerical_stability.csv), and [sample coverage](../results/tables/03_correction_annual_coverage.csv) support these qualifications.

## Forecast evidence changes the substantive ranking

M5 has the lowest Brent two-state Markov AIC, yet M4 has the lowest Markov BIC, and the held-out evidence is less favorable to incremental GPR. On validation, M5's QLIKE 2.4450 is marginally worse than M1's 2.4420 and M4's 2.4420. On the final test, M5 is better than M1 by 0.0879 QLIKE units but only 0.00079 better than M4, with paired basic intervals spanning zero in both comparisons. In WTI, M5 is numerically worse than M4 on both validation and test. Conversely, Brent M5's point QLIKE is lower than GPR-only M3 on validation and test, suggesting VIX may add more than GPR in this specification; a saved paired M3–M5 interval is unavailable, so this is not a confirmed statistical gain. These results answer H4 more narrowly than in-sample fit: GPR helps describe some fitted Brent transition patterns, but its **additional predictive value beyond VIX is unestablished**. [In-sample model table](../results/tables/04_model_comparison.csv), [Brent loss table](../results/tables/05_performance.csv), [paired comparisons](../results/tables/05_paired_block_bootstrap.csv), and [WTI loss table](../results/tables/06_wti_forecast_performance.csv) document the divergence.

The conventional benchmarks are consequential. Gaussian GARCH gives the lowest test QLIKE (2.9252) and test MSE among the seven main models; Student-t GARCH gives the lowest negative predictive log score (2.3346). This need not be contradictory. QLIKE evaluates the predicted conditional second moment against a noisy squared-return proxy, whereas the log score rewards an entire predictive density, including tail probability. The Student-t model's superior density score is consistent with the training heavy tails, although neither GARCH fit removes all residual ARCH effects and both have near-unit persistence. A general Markov superiority claim is therefore unsupported. Moreover, the wide block-bootstrap interval for the B1–M5 QLIKE difference signals that its sizeable average test gap is episodic, not necessarily a stable expected advantage. [GARCH residual diagnostics](../results/tables/03_correction_garch_residual_tests.csv), [forecast performance](../results/tables/05_performance.csv), and [paired bootstrap](../results/tables/05_paired_block_bootstrap.csv) are the record.

## Extreme conditions and sample dependence

The 2020 oil-market disruption is central to the test ranking. M5's calendar-2020 QLIKE is 6.595 against B1's 4.147, although M5 improves on M1's 7.280. The top 1% of absolute test returns contribute 17.8% of M5's aggregate QLIKE sum but only 5.3% of B1's. By 2022, the corresponding QLIKE spread is much smaller: M5 3.172 versus B1 3.127. All extreme observations remain in the reported primary metrics. These patterns show why a method's apparent average success depends on the severity and type of market shock; the Markov model's probabilistic state propagation did not protect its second-moment score as effectively as the GARCH recursion during the largest 2020 returns. The saved plots illustrate timing without serving as independent evidence of significance. [Yearly losses](../results/tables/05_test_yearly_performance.csv), [extreme-return impact](../results/tables/05_extreme_return_impact.csv), [disruption losses](../results/tables/05_disruption_performance.csv), and [disruption figure](../results/figures/05_disruption_qlike.png) support this reading.

The 2000–06 Brent missing-price pattern also matters. Estimating from 2007 reduces irregular early intervals but shortens the estimation period and changes its economic mix. Under that sensitivity, M5's validation QLIKE worsens from 2.4450 to 2.5021 and its GPR entry contrast becomes uncertain. Separately, the common-sample rule excludes 85 otherwise valid Brent returns solely because same-day VIX is unavailable, although an earlier VIX quote exists; the model uses lagged VIX. This restriction is modest relative to the early price gaps and preserves the established like-for-like comparison, but it narrows the dates represented and could affect alternative-sample estimates. The primary 2000–15 specification remains the historical benchmark, but a thesis claim that is robust to all reasonable training periods would be too strong. The shortened fit cannot distinguish whether the cause is missing early prices, lost sample size, different market episodes or an interaction of these. [Coverage audit](../results/tables/03_correction_annual_coverage.csv), [sample summary](../data/processed/01_sample_summary.csv), [Notebook 01](../notebooks/01_data_preparation.ipynb), [validation sensitivity](../results/tables/06_early_sample_performance.csv), and [transition sensitivity](../results/tables/06_transition_scenarios.csv) make these limits explicit.

## Interpretation of negative and mixed findings

The value of the design lies in separating three questions: whether latent states summarize historical returns, whether lagged indices correlate with fitted transition probabilities, and whether those indices improve one-step forecasts. They need not have the same answer. A lower in-sample AIC can reflect additional flexibility without a repeatable forecast gain. A transition contrast can be statistically distinguishable from zero yet economically modest, sample dependent or unavailable in real time. Conversely, an inexpensive GARCH recursion can forecast the second moment well even without a narrative geopolitical state variable. The negative and mixed results therefore refine the research claim: volatility regimes are descriptively useful, but more elaborate transition predictors do not earn automatic predictive preference.

The revised historical GPR series is the most important informational limitation. Its previous-calendar-day **reference value** was used, but archived publication vintages and release timestamps were not verified. Consequently, the GPR-based evaluations are historical pseudo-out-of-sample analyses despite the strict return-by-return forecast-update order. The Stage 07 AI indices were developed in 2026 and reconstructed retrospectively, and the 2020–26 period had already been examined before their comparison; that extension belongs in an appendix. None of these estimates establishes causality or a deployable real-time GPR forecasting strategy. [Original data timing](../results/tables/05_data_audit.json), [GPR source](https://www.matteoiacoviello.com/gpr.htm), and [Stage 07 metadata](../results/tables/07_source_metadata.json) support the boundary.
