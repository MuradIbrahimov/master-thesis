# Empirical Results — chapter draft

All return levels and state standard deviations below are in **percentage points**; squared returns and predicted second moments are in squared percentage points, while MSE of second-moment forecasts is in fourth powers of percentage points. QLIKE and negative log scores are computed from returns expressed in percentage points and must be compared on that same scale. Unless stated otherwise, the primary Brent training sample has 3,699 complete-case observations from 2000–2015. A lower AIC, BIC or forecast loss is preferable within a valid same-sample comparison. Retrospective smoothed probabilities and in-sample information criteria do not constitute forecast evidence.

## 1. Descriptive statistics and diagnostic tests

In the maximum usable Brent return file, the full 2000–26 sample contains 6,495 observations, with mean return 0.025, standard deviation 2.738, skewness −1.726 and excess kurtosis 64.158. WTI has 6,336 complete-case returns, standard deviation 2.848 and excess kurtosis 24.085. Those full-period figures are descriptive: they include the extreme 2020 episode and are not used for model selection. The maximum Brent training sample has 3,750 returns (standard deviation 2.342); the 3,699-observation complete-case sample is used for the principal models. WTI training returns number 3,633 (standard deviation 2.594). [Return descriptive table](../results/tables/02_return_descriptive.csv), [split table](../results/tables/02_split_descriptive.csv), and [sample summary](../data/processed/01_sample_summary.csv) supply these values. The [return-distribution figure](../results/figures/02_return_distributions.png) visually reinforces heavy tails.

Training Brent returns do not show strong linear autocorrelation at 10 lags (Ljung–Box p = 0.252), whereas squared returns do (p = 1.33×10⁻⁶³). Brent ARCH-LM at 10 lags rejects no ARCH effects (p = 1.10×10⁻³⁴). The training Jarque–Bera statistic is 5,652.98 with a p-value below numerical reporting precision, rejecting marginal normality. WTI also rejects no squared-return dependence at 10 lags (Ljung–Box p ≈ 1.00×10⁻¹⁰⁸) and no ARCH effects (p ≈ 2.71×10⁻⁵¹); unlike Brent, WTI signed returns show detectable 10-lag autocorrelation (p = 0.00866). Both return series reject the ADF unit-root null while KPSS does not reject level stationarity at the reported upper p-value bound of 0.10. These diagnostics motivate conditional-variance and regime models, but do not determine their form or the true number of states. [Ljung–Box](../results/tables/02_ljung_box.csv), [ARCH-LM](../results/tables/02_arch_lm.csv), [Jarque–Bera](../results/tables/02_jarque_bera.csv), [stationarity](../results/tables/02_stationarity.csv), and [return ACF figure](../results/figures/02_return_acf.png) are the verified sources.

The Pearson training correlation of previous-calendar-day GPR with absolute Brent return is 0.076, compared with 0.245 for previous-observation VIX. These are modest or moderate descriptive associations with magnitudes, not tests of transition mechanisms or forecasting value. Squared returns give analogous correlations 0.080 and 0.216. [Predictor correlations](../results/tables/02_predictor_correlations.csv) and [scatter figure](../results/figures/02_predictor_scatter.png) document the comparison.

The early Brent series has material price gaps. In 2000–02, raw nonmissing price counts are 202, 194 and 194, producing 197, 189 and 188 complete-case model returns. The complete-case VIX/GPR rule removes only 5, 5 and 6 otherwise usable returns, respectively; the low early counts originate primarily in the raw price file. Across the full study window, requiring a **same-day VIX observation**, even though the model predictor is lagged VIX, excludes 85 valid Brent returns (51 training, 21 validation and 13 test). All 85 had an earlier observed VIX quote in the raw file. The same-day rule is a conservative common-sample choice embedded in Notebook 01's lag construction, not a mathematical requirement of the transition equation. The retained common dates support like-for-like model comparison, but coverage and estimates may differ from a design using every return with a previous VIX quote; no such alternative model was fitted here. A return after a missing-price gap is a cumulative log price change over the recorded interval. This violates a literal evenly spaced daily interpretation of the model clock and is why the shorter training-window sensitivity is substantively relevant. [Annual coverage audit](../results/tables/03_correction_annual_coverage.csv), [Stage 03 audit](../results/tables/03_correction_summary.md), [Notebook 01](../notebooks/01_data_preparation.ipynb), [maximum Brent data](../data/processed/01_brent_max.csv) and [sample summary](../data/processed/01_sample_summary.csv) give the trace.

## 2. Constant-variance and GARCH benchmarks

On the same 3,699 training returns, B0 has AIC 16,809.46 and BIC 16,821.90; Gaussian B1 falls to 15,895.50 and 15,920.36. Student-t B1-t reduces these further to 15,715.18 and 15,746.26, with fitted degrees of freedom $\nu=6.299$. Thus conditional variance and heavier conditional tails improve *in-sample* fit. The Gaussian GARCH estimate has $\alpha+\beta\approx1.000000$ and B1-t has 0.999246, suggesting near-integrated variance persistence. This casts doubt on a simple stationary-volatility interpretation despite numerical convergence. [B0 and original comparison](../results/tables/03_model_comparison.csv), [GARCH distribution comparison](../results/tables/03_correction_garch_comparison.csv), and [Student-t parameters](../results/tables/03_correction_garch_t_parameters.csv) support the statements.

Residual checks limit the adequacy claim. Signed standardized residual Ljung–Box p-values exceed 0.1 at lags 5, 10 and 20 in both GARCH fits, but squared standardized residual Ljung–Box p-values at lag 10 are 0.000220 (Gaussian) and 0.000076 (Student-t). The corresponding ARCH-LM p-values are 0.000120 and 0.000040. Both recursions leave detectable conditional heteroskedasticity. Their forecast value must therefore be judged on held-out losses, not a claim that either has fully removed volatility clustering. [Residual test table](../results/tables/03_correction_garch_residual_tests.csv) and [fitted conditional volatility figure](../results/figures/03_garch_conditional_volatility.png) provide the evidence.

## 3. Two-state and three-state Markov models

The constant-transition M1 fits a low-volatility state with SD 1.575 and mean return +0.122, and a high-volatility state with SD 3.450 and mean −0.233. Its retrospective smoothed occupancies are 68.2% and 31.8%. The estimated stay probabilities are 0.973 and 0.944, corresponding to 37.6 and 17.7 **retained observations** at the fitted transition probabilities. All three original starts converged with negligible likelihood spread, and probability and transition normalization checks passed. The distinct fitted variances give a useful historical description, while the states remain latent and model-dependent. [M1 regime table](../results/tables/03_m1_regimes.csv), [M1 stability](../results/tables/03_markov_stability.csv), [M1 transition matrix](../results/tables/03_m1_transition_matrix.csv), and [M1 retrospective probability figure](../results/figures/03_m1_probabilities.png) are the source.

M2's three ordered state SDs are 1.055, 2.097 and 4.485, with smoothed occupancies 20.9%, 67.9% and 11.2%. Its AIC/BIC of 15,831.64/15,906.23 are lower than M1's 16,097.25/16,134.55 on the common training sample. Ten seeded starts converged with log-likelihood spread 0.00625. Yet the direct high→low transition is estimated at approximately $3.3\times10^{-20}$ and has no reported standard error. This boundary estimate prevents conventional inference on that coefficient; M2 is consequently retained as a descriptive three-level classification and omitted from the main forecast experiment. The stronger M2 fit cannot be read as proof that three true economic regimes exist. [M2 regimes](../results/tables/03_correction_m2_regimes.csv), [ten starts](../results/tables/03_correction_m2_ten_starts.csv), [parameters](../results/tables/03_correction_m2_parameters.csv), [transition matrix](../results/tables/03_correction_m2_transition_matrix.csv), and [retrospective probability figure](../results/figures/03_m2_probabilities.png) provide the audit.

## 4. GPR and VIX transition effects

M1, M3, M4 and M5 have identical Gaussian switching-mean and switching-variance observation equations on identical training dates. For M5, the fitted low/high state SDs are 1.632/3.674, with retrospective high-state occupancy 25.9%. Because the transition link parameterizes the destination-low probability, the economically relevant results are probability contrasts rather than raw coefficient signs. [Regime summary](../results/tables/04_regime_summary.csv), [M5 coefficients](../results/tables/04_m5_parameters.csv), and [transition contrasts](../results/tables/06_transition_scenarios.csv) are the source.

At training percentiles with the other predictor held at its median, moving GPR from p10 to p90 raises M5's fitted low→high probability from 1.63% to 5.19%, a **+3.565 percentage-point** contrast (delta 95% interval +1.066 to +6.064). The same change reduces high→high persistence by about 5.09 points, but its interval (−14.80, +4.61) spans zero. Thus the main GPR relationship concerns entry rather than a securely estimated persistence effect. Moving VIX from p10 to p90 raises M5's fitted high→high probability from 78.28% to 97.58%, a **+19.298-point** contrast (2.505, 36.091). Its low→high contrast is −1.295 points (−3.745, +1.155), so the evidence does not support a precise positive VIX entry effect in the primary fit. These are conditional, historical associations with transitions, not effects of an externally imposed shock. [Exact contrasts](../results/tables/06_transition_scenarios.csv) and [scenario figure](../results/figures/04_transition_scenarios.png) give the underlying probabilities.

The corresponding standardized M5 logit coefficient for GPR on low-origin→low-destination probability is −0.711 (standard error 0.258); its negative sign is consistent with *more* low→high entry. The VIX coefficient on high-origin→low-destination probability is −1.154 (standard error 0.382), consistent with *more* high→high persistence. These are log-odds coefficients and cannot be read as percentage-point changes without evaluating the fitted probability function. [M5 parameter table](../results/tables/04_m5_parameters.csv) records the estimates and standard errors.

Some daily transition probabilities become very close to zero or one. For M5, the smallest training transition probability in the saved Stage 04 summary is about $3.6\times10^{-5}$; the validation fit in Stage 05 has a similar minimum. The row-sum and bound checks pass, but local uncertainty near such extremes and covariance-based intervals deserve caution. [Transition summary](../results/tables/04_average_transitions.csv) and [fit diagnostics](../results/tables/05_fit_diagnostics.csv) are the sources.

## 5. In-sample model comparison

Within the four same-sample two-state Markov models, M5 has the lowest AIC (16,071.18), followed by M4 (16,075.99), M3 (16,087.02) and M1 (16,097.25). BIC instead prefers M4 (16,125.72) to M5 (16,133.34), reflecting the larger transition equation. AIC and BIC therefore do not yield a unanimous choice about GPR's incremental value. They describe likelihood fit with a parameter penalty and cannot establish a held-out gain. [Stage 04 comparison](../results/tables/04_model_comparison.csv) contains likelihoods, parameters, convergence and sample counts.

Across B0, GARCH and Markov models, the in-sample criteria are presented as descriptive benchmarks: GARCH likelihood recursions and Markov state likelihoods initialize differently, and model classes encode different distributional assumptions. Their forecast comparison below is the decision-relevant evidence. [Stage 03 comparison](../results/tables/03_model_comparison.csv) and [Student-t supplement](../results/tables/03_correction_garch_comparison.csv) identify the common observation count.

## 6. Out-of-sample forecasting results

The table reproduces the common-date losses in [05_performance.csv](../results/tables/05_performance.csv). Validation uses 999 returns in 2016–19 and training-only estimates. Test uses 1,712 returns from 2020–26 October and parameters re-estimated only through 2019. QLIKE and MSE evaluate predicted conditional **second moments** against squared returns; negative log score evaluates the full predictive density. Lower values are better.

| Model | Validation QLIKE | Validation MSE | Validation negative log score | Test QLIKE | Test MSE | Test negative log score |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| B0 | 2.5647 | 97.165 | 2.2012 | 4.2258 | 13,519.26 | 3.0318 |
| B1 Gaussian GARCH | 2.4463 | 91.481 | 2.1420 | **2.9252** | **12,750.01** | 2.3816 |
| B1-t Student-t GARCH | 2.4486 | 91.451 | **2.1243** | 2.9442 | 12,792.54 | **2.3346** |
| M1 constant transitions | **2.4420** | 90.683 | 2.1327 | 3.4235 | 13,434.26 | 2.5855 |
| M3 GPR transitions | 2.4478 | 91.008 | 2.1358 | 3.3780 | 13,422.76 | 2.5639 |
| M4 VIX transitions | 2.4420 | **89.642** | 2.1310 | 3.3364 | 13,411.16 | 2.5531 |
| M5 GPR + VIX | 2.4450 | 89.724 | 2.1323 | 3.3356 | 13,410.00 | 2.5503 |

Validation QLIKE gives M1 and M4 a nearly identical numerical lead; M5 is slightly worse. In the final test M5 gains 0.0879 QLIKE units on M1, but M4 and M5 are separated by only 0.000790. Gaussian GARCH has the lowest test QLIKE and MSE, while Student-t GARCH has the best test density score. This metric divergence is substantive: a model may forecast the squared-return magnitude relatively well yet assign less probability to extreme realized returns. [Actual versus forecast](../results/figures/05_actual_vs_forecast.png), [predicted volatility](../results/figures/05_predicted_volatility.png), and [cumulative QLIKE differences](../results/figures/05_cumulative_qlike_difference.png) show the time profile rather than changing the numerical comparison.

The actual-versus-forecast figure caps the **displayed** squared-return axis at its 99th percentile for readability; the underlying 2020 extremes remain in every saved loss calculation. [Reproducibility metadata](../results/tables/05_reproducibility.json) records that plot-only choice.

Paired circular-block bootstrap results temper point rankings. M1−M5 test mean QLIKE difference is +0.087905 with basic 95% interval (−0.04730, +0.18040); the corresponding validation difference is −0.003005 (−0.02084, +0.01688). M4−M5 test difference is +0.000790 (−0.01497, +0.01309). B1−M5 test difference is −0.410456 (−0.80895, +0.28903), also with a wide interval spanning zero. Thus neither a reliable M5 improvement over M1 nor an incremental GPR benefit over M4 is established, and even B1's large average QLIKE edge is episodically uncertain under the chosen resampling design. The table's centered p-values are secondary to the stated intervals and should not be presented as confirmatory tests after multiple comparisons. [Paired bootstrap table](../results/tables/05_paired_block_bootstrap.csv) records all paired comparisons and settings.

The 2020 calendar year makes the aggregate contrast unusually sensitive to tail events. Its QLIKE is 4.147 for B1, 7.280 for M1, 6.647 for M4 and 6.595 for M5. In 2022 the same figures are 3.127, 3.192, 3.172 and 3.172, a much narrower spread. The 18 largest absolute Brent returns in the test (top 1%) account for 17.8% of M5's sum of QLIKE losses but 5.3% of B1's; 2020 contributes 28.9% and 20.7%, respectively. Excluding those dates is **diagnostic only**; all remain in the primary test scores. The result suggests that M5's higher aggregate test loss relative to GARCH is concentrated in extreme shocks and that general superiority cannot be inferred from an average alone. [Calendar-year table](../results/tables/05_test_yearly_performance.csv), [disruption table](../results/tables/05_disruption_performance.csv), [extreme-return influence](../results/tables/05_extreme_return_impact.csv), [yearly figure](../results/figures/05_yearly_qlike.png), and [disruption figure](../results/figures/05_disruption_qlike.png) document the predetermined windows.

The forecasting pipeline used three successful independent starts for each two-state Markov fit in both estimation windows; the maximum recorded Stage 05 Markov likelihood spread is below 0.0001. The 28 primary fit attempts recorded no estimation warning or failure, and the forecasts passed timing, state-sum, positive-moment and common-date checks. Both GARCH refits remain near integrated: test-fit $\alpha+\beta=0.998955$ for B1 and 0.998347 for B1-t. Numerically successful evaluation therefore does not resolve GARCH persistence, extreme-event sensitivity or revised-GPR timing. [Fit attempts](../results/tables/05_fit_attempts.csv), [fit diagnostics](../results/tables/05_fit_diagnostics.csv), and [reproducibility record](../results/tables/05_reproducibility.json) give the verification.

## 7. WTI robustness

WTI M1 again separates fitted low/high state SDs, 1.800 and 4.445. In a WTI M5 fit, the GPR p90−p10 low→high contrast is +1.245 percentage points but its delta interval (−0.536, +3.027) spans zero. The VIX p90−p10 high→high contrast is +21.053 points (−0.087, +42.194), directionally similar to Brent but imprecise at this specification. WTI M4 alone gives a +21.343-point high→high VIX contrast (1.186, 41.501). Thus the low/high variance description transports better than the precision of the two-predictor transition effects. [WTI regimes](../results/tables/06_regime_summary.csv) and [WTI transition scenarios](../results/tables/06_transition_scenarios.csv) are the sources.

WTI validation QLIKE values are 2.5179 (M1), 2.5206 (M4) and 2.5229 (M5); test QLIKE values are 3.1401, 3.1031 and 3.1065. M4 has a numerical test advantage over M1, whereas adding GPR slightly worsens M4. The original Stage 06 M1−M4 test mean difference is +0.036959, and its basic equal-tail 95% interval (−0.005712, +0.067769) includes zero. The original centered absolute-tail p = 0.0475 uses a different two-sided convention; the equal-tail two-sided p **consistent with that interval** is **0.0820**. The manuscript reports the latter with the interval and does not declare a 5% WTI M4 improvement. This reporting correction does not alter forecasts or the Stage 06 raw table. Multiple paired comparisons were examined, so even an isolated small p-value would require caution. [WTI performance](../results/tables/06_wti_forecast_performance.csv), [original bootstrap](../results/tables/06_wti_paired_bootstrap.csv), and [correction](../results/tables/07_stage06_bootstrap_correction.csv) provide exact values. Notebook 01's previous-observed-price rule leaves the 20 and 21 April 2020 WTI log returns undefined but retains 22 April's +42.583% return; none is silently imputed. [Price audit](../data/processed/01_wti_price_return_audit.csv) confirms the three dates.

## 8. Alternative lags, GPR transformation and sample sensitivity

The Brent five-calendar-day GPR-lag M5 validation QLIKE is 2.4443 versus 2.4450 for the original lag; its p90−p10 GPR low→high contrast remains positive at +4.049 points (1.231, 6.866). A five-previous-observation VIX lag gives M5 QLIKE 2.4456 and a +14.616-point (2.385, 26.848) high→high VIX contrast. Replacing the GPR level with $\log(1+\mathrm{GPR})$ gives M5 validation QLIKE 2.4453, while its GPR low→high contrast changes to +5.977 points (2.421, 9.532). The forecast ranking is nearly unchanged on the same 999 validation dates, while probability-effect size depends on transformation and lag. [Lag performance](../results/tables/06_alternative_lag_performance.csv), [transform performance](../results/tables/06_gpr_transform_performance.csv), and [transition effects](../results/tables/06_transition_scenarios.csv) verify this sensitivity.

Starting the Brent fit in 2007 retains a low/high variance ordering but weakens the main GPR entry contrast to +0.980 points (−7.207, +9.167). M5 validation QLIKE deteriorates from 2.4450 to 2.5021, and M4 from 2.4420 to 2.5027, on identical validation dates; M1 changes only from 2.4420 to 2.4463. The shorter sample avoids the worst 2000–06 price coverage, but it also removes seven years of information and changes the training distribution, so the difference cannot isolate the effect of missing days alone. It establishes sample dependence, not which training period is objectively correct. [2007-start validation performance](../results/tables/05_2007_start_validation_performance.csv), [early-sample comparison](../results/tables/06_early_sample_performance.csv), [short-sample effects](../results/tables/06_transition_scenarios.csv), and [robustness figure](../results/figures/06_transition_effect_robustness.png) provide the record.
